Case study · Data engineering & semantic layer

The source of truth was a 40-tab spreadsheet.

I replaced it with a governed daily mart (eight sources, one row per day, ~130 documented metrics) that reconciles to finance within dollars and now catches broken data feeds before anyone presents a wrong number.

When2026 · ~5 months, alongside delivery work
RoleSole designer & builder
Stackdbt · BigQuery · Python · scheduled queries
Scale8 sources · 9 staging models · ~130 daily metrics · ~980 days of history

The situation

Answering anything about the ecommerce business meant joining eight-plus source tables across different warehouse projects: ERP sales, two web-analytics feeds, behavioral sessions, media spend, search-ads data, the contact center, store foot traffic. Every new dashboard started with the same 200-plus lines of SQL, subtly re-derived each time. The same metric carried different definitions in different reports, and the de-facto arbiter was a hand-updated, 40-tab workbook that one person maintained daily.

The predictable result: meetings that opened with a debate about whose number was right, before anyone could get to what the number meant.

What I did

The engineering is boring on purpose: one staging model per source, typed and tested; everything joined to the fiscal calendar so a missing day is visible instead of silent; a single mart table with one row per day and ~130 metrics whose definitions live in the warehouse UI, not in tribal memory. Production runs on scheduled queries each morning and rewrites a trailing seven-day window on every run, so late-arriving source data corrects itself without anyone noticing there was a problem.

The hard part wasn't the SQL. It was making the definitions match finance. What counts as an ecommerce sale, which of a thousand legacy store codes belong to the channel, how vendor income behaves on a return: each of those was an argument until it was a reconciliation. The channel-definition fight alone moved a quarterly figure by millions; settling it against the finance system brought the gap from seven figures down to double-digit dollars on an eight-figure quarter. After that, the debates stopped, not because anyone won but because there was nothing left to debate.

The turn

The surprise came after launch: the mart turned out to be less valuable as plumbing than as an alarm system. When one table carries every source side by side, a broken feed can't hide.

Three catches in one summer: a media-spend feed silently zeroed its newest day, which would have turned a soft week into a fake disaster. The mart's cross-source spend columns flagged the gap before the weekly readout. A web-analytics feed began partially loading and sessions "fell" 90%, while the parallel behavioral source showed a normal seasonal dip, proving a feed failure rather than a traffic collapse, and a staleness alert shipped that week. And a units-per-transaction metric turned out to be inflated roughly 70% by free promotional items nobody had filtered.

A semantic layer isn't just where the numbers agree. It's where the broken feeds get caught before they reach a deck.

What happened after

More than 25 downstream projects now read the mart: the weekly executive deck, the dashboard suite, all three forecast engines, and most new analysis. The deck's canonical forecast switched from a stale planning file, which had drifted roughly ten points from reality, to the mart's daily forecast columns. Hard-coded stopgap numbers in the deck were deleted once the mart reproduced them to within basis points. And the 40-tab workbook was retired from its role as referee.

The quieter payoff: analyses like the organic-decline decomposition could treat sessions as ground truth precisely because the ground truth had already been built.

Technical notes, for the people who will ask

Modeling. dbt over BigQuery: sources → one staging model per source (typed, deduplicated, calendar-joined) → a single wide daily mart. LEFT JOINs from the calendar spine so no source can drop a row; ~3.5K lines of SQL, ~5K of Python for the API pulls; a 20-test validation suite on the trickiest feed.

Operations. Production runs as warehouse-scheduled queries (staging, then mart) every morning, DELETE+INSERT over a trailing 7-day window, so runs are idempotent and late data self-heals. API ingestion retries on a scheduler; metric documentation is pushed into the warehouse schema UI so definitions travel with the table.

Fiscal-calendar correctness. Year-over-year comparisons use a mapped last-year date column rather than a naive 364-day offset; the retail calendar's 53-week years and moving holidays break the naive version in ways that only surface at the worst possible time.

Resilience. Overlapping sources are kept side by side (two web-analytics feeds, spend from platform and API) with blended columns that prefer the freshest complete source. That redundancy is what makes the feed-failure catches possible.
