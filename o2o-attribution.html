Case study · Attribution & measurement

Paid marketing looked unprofitable until we counted store sales.

Web analytics graded the paid portfolio below break-even because it could not see the stores. Identity-stitched store transactions and channel-level holdout tests put the true incremental return near 4x, and budget allocation now runs on it.

When2025–2026 · in production
RoleLed a 3-person build · architecture & pipelines mine
DataBehavioral sessions · tag events · omni orders · ERP store transactions
MethodsIdentity stitching · tiered last-touch · channel holdouts · ERP calibration

The situation

At a roughly 2,300-store retailer, most transactions happen in stores while most journeys start online. Channel credit came from single-session, web-only last-click, which fails twice. It misassigns even online sales: a customer who arrived through brand search four times and returned via a bookmark scores as Direct. And it cannot see store sales at all, so marketing was graded on the minority of revenue its channels touched. The ad platforms were no help; their store metrics optimize toward modeled store visits, not sales.

Leadership needed channel returns they could allocate budget against: which marketing dollars actually produce store sales, and how much of that would have happened anyway?

What I did

I led a three-person build, directing a tagging expert on event capture, partnering with a developer on integration, and building the data model, pipelines, and stitching logic myself. Identity first: visitor identifiers are extracted from the behavioral stream, linked across devices and sessions, and joined to orders assembled from three overlapping sources so a single broken tag cannot silently drop sales. Journey second: each converter gets a traced path over a 30-day lookback, and credit is assigned by tiered last-touch across 16 channels (paid touches outrank earned, earned outrank fallback, and the last touch in the highest tier present wins), so a final direct visit cannot steal credit from the search ad that started the journey.

Then the discipline that made it trustworthy: stitched channel proportions are calibrated to ERP totals, so credited dollars foot to finance, and the stitch rates themselves are published on the dashboard rather than buried. High-80s percent of ecommerce orders carry a full journey; store orders stitch in the high teens; and nearly half of store orders carry no digital identity at all, so they are credited to no one. The last layer turns attribution into incrementality: rotating matched-market holdout tests, run channel by channel, measure how much of the attributed revenue would have happened anyway, and those factors calibrate the reported returns. Brand search took the biggest haircut, because raw last-touch credit flatters the channel people search for on purpose. Click-based credit still makes awareness channels a lower bound, and the dashboard says so.

The turn

The first omni report landed like a verdict. On web-only numbers, blended paid ROAS read about 0.9: the entire portfolio, apparently underwater. With store sales attached and the holdout calibration applied, it read about 4. The channels moved unevenly, and that unevenness is what changed decisions.

On the old report, the ads looked like they were losing money. Most of them were funding the stores.

Two smaller corrections mattered almost as much. A model in the stack that claimed to be Shapley turned out not to be: its coalition math made every marginal contribution negative, so thousands of multi-channel journeys silently fell through to an equal split. I rebuilt it, then rejected the rebuilds on principle and relabeled the method honestly. And a converter definition that required a payment page was quietly dropping orders paid through wallet redirects; fixing it lifted ecommerce order capture from roughly 70% to 95%.

What happened after

The stitched pipeline became the house source of truth for channel revenue and ROAS, with a standing rule that other dashboards' money rows must foot to it to the dollar, because stakeholders add numbers up across reports and a bridge that reads lower is dead on arrival. It refreshes incrementally every day; ecommerce reads next-morning while store credit waits out the match window rather than reporting prematurely. Channel budget allocation now runs on the holdout-calibrated incremental returns, with media mix modeling kept alongside as the long-horizon check on upper-funnel effects. Where a holdout and the attribution disagree about a channel, the disagreement is documented and the holdout wins.

Technical notes, for the people who will ask

Identity. Visitor identifiers extracted from the behavioral stream and joined across linked devices; a later layer adds hashed-email and analytics-ID edges built by co-occurrence, with guards (identities shared across several distinct emails are dropped; bot networks filtered). Orders come from three overlapping sources unioned, because each source covers the others' failure modes.

Credit rules. 30-day lookback on sessions at or before the order timestamp; tiered last-touch (paid, then earned, then fallback tiers) across 16 channels; converters with no attributable touch are redistributed proportionally rather than dumped into Direct. Direct itself is documented as inflated by browser privacy features, with the evidence attached.

Calibration. Stitched proportions scale to ERP totals by order channel, and a year-over-year stitch-improvement correction prevents better stitching from masquerading as revenue growth. Data-completeness flags mark provisional days instead of letting them look final.

Incrementality. Rotating matched-market holdouts by channel: comparable markets go dark on one channel for several weeks while their matches keep spending, and the sales gap yields the per-channel incrementality factor that calibrates attributed returns. Cross-checks agree: an alternative attribution model matches tiered last-touch on awareness share to within tenths of a point, and a third-party affiliate network agrees on about 70% of contested converters, with the disagreement set characterized rather than hidden.
